It treats ideas as hypotheses to be evaluated rather than assumptions to be implemented. This ethos encourages teams to explore possibilities early, expose weaknesses quickly and let evidence—not intuition—shape the direction of a product. Purpose of Prototyping Prototyping exists to uncover the truth about an idea before committing resources to full development. It helps teams understand whether a concept is desirable, feasible and viable by turning abstract thinking into something concrete enough to evaluate. Reduce uncertainty by validating assumptions early. Reveal risks—technical, usability, business—before they become expensive. Accelerate learning through quick cycles of build–test–refine. Align stakeholders around a shared understanding of what is being built. Prototyping Supports Product Discovery Prototyping is central to discovery because it transforms exploration into actionable insight. Instead of debating ideas in the abstract, teams can observe real reactions and real behavio u r. User insight: prototypes make it easier to gather meaningful feedback because users can interact with something tangible. Team clarity: prototypes clarify intent, reduce misinterpretation and help teams converge on a shared vision. Decision-making: evidence from prototypes guides prioritization and helps determine which ideas deserve further investment. Forms of Prototypes Various levels of fidelity serve different purposes and choosing the right form is part of the ethos. Low ‑ fidelity: sketches, wireframes, storyboards — ideal for exploring concepts and workflows. Mid ‑ fidelity: clickable mock-ups or simplified functional models — useful for testing usability and flow. High ‑ fidelity: near ‑ real interactions or technical spikes — used to validate feasibility or performance. Each level is intentionally disposable; the value lies in what is learned, not in the artifact itself. Prototypes as Communication Tools Prototypes bridge gaps between disciplines—design, engineering, product and stakeholders—by making ideas visible and concrete. They clarify intent better than documents or verbal descriptions. They surface misunderstandings early when they are cheap to fix. They enable collaboration, giving everyone a shared reference point. This communication function is often as valuable as the testing itself. Risk Reduction Through Early Exposure Prototyping reduces risk by exposing assumptions to reality. It helps teams identify: Technical : performance limits, integration challenges, architectural constraints. User : unclear value, confusing interactions, unmet expectations. Business : weak demand, misaligned pricing, operational complexity. By confronting these risks early, teams avoid costly rework and increase the likelihood of delivering a successful product. Cultural Principles Behind the Ethos A strong prototyping culture is built on a few core beliefs: Progress over perfection: prototypes are meant to be rough, fast and disposable. Learning over certainty: the goal is insight, not validation of preconceived ideas. Collaboration over silos: prototypes invite participation across roles. Evidence over opinion: decisions are grounded in what users do, not what teams assume. These principles help teams stay adaptable, curious and aligned. User Base Overview The application will begin as a single ‑ user system, designed to support one operational environment, one set of credentials and one configuration context. This initial scope allows rapid prototyping, faster iteration and reduced architectural overhead during early development. As the product matures beyond the prototype stage, the system will transition to a multi ‑ user, multi ‑ tenant architecture capable of supporting independent user environments, isolated configurations and scalable integrations. Initial Single ‑ User Phase The early development phase focuses on validating core functionality without the complexity of user management or tenant isolation. The system will operate with: A single API key or credential set One broker integration A single instrument mapping configuration Local or simplified configuration storage Minimal external service integrations This phase prioritizes speed, experimentation and functional correctness. It also establishes the baseline workflows and data structures that will later need to scale. Transition to Multi ‑ User Architecture Once the prototype has validated the core concept, the application will evolve to support multiple users, each with their own operational context. This transition introduces requirements for identity, access control, configuration isolation and scalable integration management. Key changes include: User authentication and authorization: introduction of user accounts, roles and permissions. Tenant isolation : separation of user data, configurations and operational environments to prevent cross ‑ contamination. Scalable configuration storage: structured storage for user ‑ specific settings, credentials and preferences. Concurrency handling : ensuring that multiple users can operate simultaneously without conflict or performance degradation. This stage requires architectural adjustments to support secure, dependable and maintainable multi ‑ user operation. Multi ‑ User Requirements and Expansion Areas As the system grows, several components must be redesigned or extended to support independent user environments and integrations. Multiple API keys: each user may require unique credentials for external services, necessitating secure storage, rotation and validation mechanisms. Multiple broker connections: users may connect to different brokers or accounts, requiring modular integration layers and dynamic routing. Instrument mapping per broker : each broker will define their own symbol mappings or trading universes, requiring isolated configuration sets. Business intelligence reporting: reporting must support user ‑ specific datasets, permissions and dashboards. Application configuration management: system ‑ wide settings must be separated from user ‑ level configurations, with clear precedence rules. Mail configuration : users may define their own SMTP or notification preferences, requiring per ‑ user communication profiles. Slack integration : support for user ‑ specific Slack webhooks or channels, with secure credential handling. Telegram integration : per ‑ user bot tokens or chat IDs, with message routing based on user context. These capabilities collectively form the foundation of a robust multi ‑ tenant system. Architectural Considerations Supporting multi ‑ user functionality introduces several architectural implications that should be addressed early in the design phase. Data isolation : database schemas or partitioning strategies must ensure user data separation. Security model : encryption, credential vaulting and access control become mandatory. Scalability : the system must manage increased load from multiple concurrent users and integrations . Extensibility : integration layers should be modular to support new brokers, notification channels, or reporting tools. Auditability : user ‑ level logging and traceability become essential for debugging and compliance. These considerations help ensure that the system can grow without requiring major re ‑ architecture later. Knowledge sources Commitment of Traders COT reports show how different market participants—commercial hedgers, large speculators, and small traders—are positioned in futures markets. Traders use it to understand long term sentiment, spot accumulation or distribution phases, and identify when positioning becomes extreme. Its main limitation is that it updates weekly, so it is not suitable for short term timing or intraday signals. X (Twitter) X provides real ‑ time market sentiment, breaking news, and commentary from analysts, traders, and institutions. It is valuable for gauging crowd psychology, monitoring macro events, and spotting sudden shifts in risk appetite. Its weakness is noise: information quality varies widely, and emotional or misleading posts can distort perception if not filtered carefully. Financial Times The Financial Times is a high-quality source of global macroeconomic news, policy developments, and institutional market analysis. It provides context on interest rates, inflation trends, geopolitical risks, and corporate activity, all of which influence broader market direction. Traders use it to build a macro narrative and understand the “why” behind market moves. Its limitation is that it is not designed for trading signals or immediacy, so it is better suited for strategic awareness rather than execution timing. Metals: Chicago Mercantile Exchange The CME metals market (including gold, silver, and copper futures) provides insight into global commodity sentiment, inflation expectations, and macroeconomic positioning. Traders use CME data to track futures pricing, volume, and open interest, which can indicate whether moves are supported by strong participation or weakening momentum. It is also valuable for understanding how institutional players are positioning around key macro events such as interest rate decisions or geopolitical risks. Metals—especially gold—often function as a barometer for risk sentiment and currency strength, particularly the US dollar. Its limitation is that futures markets can be influenced by leverage and short-term flows, so price movements may not always reflect longer-term fundamentals without confirmation. Equities: Director Transactions Director transactions track insider buying and selling activity within companies. Significant insider buying can indicate confidence in future performance, while selling may signal caution or profit-taking. Traders use this information as a sentiment and confirmation tool alongside technical and fundamental analysis. Its limitation is that insider actions can have multiple motivations and do not always lead to immediate price movement. Equities: Short Positions (FCA) Short interest and disclosed short positions provide insight into bearish sentiment toward specific assets. High short interest can indicate underlying weakness or scepticism , but it can also create conditions for short squeezes if price moves sharply higher. Traders use this data to understand positioning risk and potential volatility. Its limitation is that timing remains uncertain, and crowded shorts can persist longer than expected. Equities: Broker Recommendations Broker recommendations include analyst upgrades, downgrades, and price target changes issued by financial institutions. These can influence short-term price movements due to shifts in institutional sentiment and market perception. Traders monitor clusters of upgrades or downgrades for momentum opportunities. However, these recommendations can lag price action and may reflect herd behaviour, so they should not be followed blindly. London Stock Exchange The London Stock Exchange is a primary venue for UK equities and provides real-time price action, order flow, and liquidity insight into listed companies. Traders use it to monitor opening auctions, intraday volume patterns, and closing imbalances, all of which can signal institutional activity. It is particularly useful for identifying how UK-specific news, earnings, or macro developments are being priced in real time. Additionally, observing sector-wide movements on the exchange can help confirm broader themes such as rotation or risk-on/risk-off sentiment. Its limitation is that it reflects price rather than explains it, so it must be used alongside news and contextual analysis.
